01 / Ontology

An ontology is a shared mental model —
for machines.

Think of an ontology as the vocabulary and grammar of your business, written in a form a computer can reason over.

When an actuary says “a 2014-vintage MYGA block in surrender period three with elevated lapse experience in the hot-money cohort, ceded 50% on a funds-withheld basis to a Bermuda affiliate,” every term is loaded with meaning — and every term connects to other concepts. Vintage implies surrender schedule. Cohort implies behavior. Cession structure implies which RBC charges transfer and which stay. The actuary’s brain holds this web of concepts.

An ontology makes that web explicit. It names the classes (Block, CreditingRateTier, RBCCharge, ReinsStructure), the properties (account value, duration, lapse curve, ceding commission), and the relationships (a Block hasTier, generates RBCCharges, is reinsured under a Treaty).

Without an ontology, an LLM knows the words. With an ontology, the system knows the meaning — and can reason over it deterministically.

Once the world is modeled this way, rules become composable and inheritable. A guardrail that applies to “all long-duration spread products” automatically applies to anything the ontology classifies as such — MYGA, FIA, ULSG. Write the rule once, against the concept. And ontologies compose across domains: the same Block concept anchors ALM, capital management, reinsurance evaluation, and acquisition analysis.

The same Block concept anchors ALM, capital management, reinsurance evaluation, and acquisition analysis. One in-force position resolves to one entity everywhere — the foundation of clean cross-entity capital optimization.
02 / L&A: Current State

What’s at stake.
Precisely.

At a typical $15–20B life and annuity carrier, the following categories of value are recurring, quantifiable, and currently undercaptured.

01
Reinvestment timing
Maturities roll into a market that has moved. New money goes to work weeks later than it could, and on a $20B general account, even 30bps of reinvestment lag costs roughly $6M annually per percent of book turning over. At normal turnover rates of 8–12% per year, the recurring annual cost sits in the $10–20M range.
02
Spread dislocation capture
When spreads widen, carriers with sufficient liquidity and authorization buy. Carriers running quarterly approval cycles miss the window. The differential is permanent NII for the next decade on every dollar deployed. A two-week cycle delay during a 50bps dislocation event costs roughly $5–10M of locked-in annual spread on a $300M deployment sleeve, compounding forward for the life of the position.
03
Liability repricing signals
Lapse signals are pricing signals. Elevated surrenders in a fixed annuity book are not just a liquidity event — they are a segmentation signal. A carrier that reprices crediting-rate tiers in response to lapse cohort data captures $5–8M annually on a typical fixed annuity book relative to one running a uniform repricing calendar.
04
Capital velocity
Reinsurance evaluations that take six months miss favorable pricing windows. Capital trapped in legacy in-force blocks for an extra two quarters is capital not redeploying at hurdle rates. On a $4B legacy block, two quarters of delay on a 50% cession represents approximately $35M of foregone redeployment income — capital present, but idle, while the market moves.
05
PRT capital efficiency
Pension risk transfer is structurally attractive — long-duration assets that fit GA portfolios, limited competition at the top, and mortality improvement that accrues to the carrier. PRT generates among the highest NII per dollar of RBC deployed in the product mix. A 10-percentage-point improvement in win rate is worth $8–15M annually for an active writer, fully utilizing existing capital.
06
Continuous cross-entity optimization
Multi-entity carriers have processes for entity routing, but those processes were built for a quarterly cadence and a static capital snapshot. NSAI runs this continuously, across all live positions simultaneously. At a multi-entity $20B carrier, static quarterly routing creates $5–15M of annual ROE drag.

What carriers already have

Sophisticated tools.
Sophisticated teams.

Most large life and annuity carriers do not lack capital management sophistication. This is not a market of unsophisticated actors waiting to be taught ALM.

ActuarialProphet, AXIS, MG-ALFA, Polysystems, RAFM, GGY-AXIS — often multiple in parallel, heavily customized over decades to match the specific liability structure of the company.
Asset managementAladdin, BlackRock Solutions, or proprietary systems managing duration targets, sector allocation, and daily liquidity across a general account spanning dozens of asset classes.
Capital modelingCustom internal RBC and economic capital tools, sometimes built over fifteen years by teams that know the book better than any vendor does.
ALM functionDedicated, often led by senior actuaries with 20+ years of experience and deep familiarity with the specific liability profile of the company.
GovernanceBoard-level capital management committees with independent actuarial review, rating agency relationships, and regulatory reporting infrastructure already in place.

These are not gaps. Anyone arguing that large L&A carriers need to be taught capital management is not a credible counterparty — and will be recognized as such immediately.

But the question is not whether carriers have capability. The question is whether that capability is integrated, fast, and auditable enough to capture the value identified above.
